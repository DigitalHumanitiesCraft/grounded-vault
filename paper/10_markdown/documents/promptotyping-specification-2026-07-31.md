# Promptotyping. Translating Research Data into Research Artefacts through Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering

Scope: chapters 1 and 2 of the method paper, the parts that define the method. Chapters 3 and 4, the abstract, the note on AI use and the reference list are not represented here. Source state: version 0.9, review draft of 31 July 2026, taken from `knowledge/paper.md` in the repository DigitalHumanitiesCraft/Promptotyping and published at <https://dhcraft.org/Promptotyping/>. Footnote markers are kept as they stand in the original; the footnote definitions live at the end of the full paper and are not part of this representation.

## 1. Introduction

Scholarly work with digital research data is necessarily mediated by software. Research activities such as enrichment and analysis may require the operationalisation of selected research concepts through procedures that make their instances identifiable, measurable, or available for downstream processing (Pichler and Reiter 2022). More broadly, enrichment, analysis, and dissemination[^1] rely on representations and procedures through which modelled distinctions become processable, queryable, and presentable by software. A CSV file can be opened as searchable text, interpreted as a sortable table in a spreadsheet, or imported into *Gephi*[^2] as a node or edge table when its records and attributes conform to structures the software can process. Relations encoded in project-specific TEI,[^3] by contrast, can be represented as a network only after relevant entities, relations, and uncertainties have been interpreted and mapped to a graph model and a compatible input format. A tool such as *Gephi* can process only those distinctions that have been expressed through graph structures and attributes supported by its representational and operational model. Project-specific semantics and uncertainty can therefore inform software-supported analysis and visualisation only insofar as they have been encoded in structures that the tool can process. ^p001

Large language models (LLMs)[^4] can reduce parts of the effort required both to map research data to the models and formats expected by existing software and to implement more closely adapted ways of working with those data. Structured or sufficiently semi-structured data can often be transformed deterministically through an explicit mapping. Where the relevant structure is not yet explicit, it must first be recognised or interpreted. If an LLM or another machine-learning system performs this step, the resulting output is probabilistic. Generative models can accordingly be used at different stages of research workflows in which digital research data are created, transformed, enriched, analysed, or made accessible (Pollin et al. 2025). They may generate structured output, produce code for deterministic transformations, or combine probabilistic and deterministic operations within a workflow designed for the material and task.[^5] ^p002

Mapping data into an existing tool may require less implementation effort than developing a project-specific artefact, but it also confines subsequent work to the distinctions, operations, and forms of interaction that the tool provides. LLM-based code generation can reduce parts of the implementation effort that have traditionally made project-specific development difficult for individual researchers and small projects. It can therefore make it practicable to develop and compare a wider range of project-specific digital research artefacts, allowing software to be adapted more closely to particular research data, questions, and scholarly practices rather than requiring those practices to conform exclusively to generic tools. ^p003

These possibilities do not eliminate the need for verification and validation. Probabilistic model outputs may be unsupported or internally inconsistent, while deterministic transformations may be incorrectly specified or implemented. Technical verification assesses whether an output or implementation conforms to formalised requirements, such as a schema, test, constraint, or specified mapping. Scholarly validation assesses whether the underlying representation is warranted by the source material and adequate for its intended research purpose.[^6] An implementation may therefore be technically correct while failing scholarly validation because the requirement or mapping it faithfully realises is itself inadequate. ^p004

This paper introduces *Promptotyping*, an iterative, knowledge-driven method for developing project-specific digital research artefacts from structured research data and maintained project knowledge. The name combines *prompt* and *prototyping* and retains the established function of a prototype as a provisional implementation through which requirements and design possibilities can be examined and refined. Such artefacts may take different forms, including interfaces, transformations, workflows, and research tools designed to support particular scholarly questions, data models, and practices. **The paper asks how scholars, research software engineers, and AI agents can collaborate through documented project knowledge to iteratively develop project-specific digital research artefacts that help project contributors clarify requirements and examine which forms of scholarly work the project’s research data can support.** Section 1 formulates the problem and establishes the conceptual and technical basis for the method. Section 2 presents the method itself, including its knowledge architecture, recurrent forms of work, and forms of verification, validation, and acceptance, and examines its operation through complementary project cases. It also defines the *promptotype*, the identifiable and versioned state that an accepted iteration yields. Section 3 compares the wider documented corpus to identify recurring project conditions, methodological functions, and artefact forms, and examines the epistemic consequences of artefact-mediated scholarly enquiry. Section 4 defines the method’s evidential scope, the conditions of its responsible application, and priorities for evaluation and transfer, and concludes by returning to the problem with which the paper began. ^p005

### 1.1 Translating Research Data into Digital Research Artefacts through Scholar-Centred Design

*Digital research data* broadly include digitally encoded materials collected, created, transformed, or used in the course of research. The term does not designate a uniform or self-evident class of objects. What counts as research data depends on the disciplinary and project context in which material is selected and interpreted (Geiger 2024). This paper focuses on structured research data: selective representations in which particular aspects of research material are made computationally explicit and thereby available for defined operations. This dependence on selection and interpretation is particularly consequential in the humanities, where historical and cultural sources often resist complete or unambiguous representation. Features of these sources become addressable only through scholarly modelling decisions, including decisions about how entities are related and how missing or uncertain information is represented (Drucker 2011; Schöch 2013). Although research data may themselves be understood as digital research artefacts in a broad sense, this paper distinguishes the two by their function within the research process. ***Digital research artefacts*** **are project-specific operational forms through which researchers work with structured research data for a defined scholarly purpose.** ^p006

Modelling provides a basis for this operationalisation by making selected aspects of a research domain explicit and relating them to a particular scholarly purpose (Ciula et al. 2023). It may involve conceptual accounts of the domain and research context as well as formal data models that specify which distinctions can be represented and processed computationally (Flanders and Jannidis 2019). These distinctions may be expressed through project-specific models, community standards such as TEI, or Semantic Web technologies.[^7] Such formalisation can contribute to FAIR preparation (Wilkinson et al. 2016) by supporting the machine-actionability, interoperability, and reuse of research data across systems.[^8] It does not, however, determine how researchers should work with those data in a particular project. The same data may support different research questions and therefore require different operational forms. ^p007

Digital tool criticism has accordingly shown that software participates in scholarly knowledge production rather than serving as a neutral means of access (van Es, Wieringa, and Schäfer 2018; Koolen, van Gorp, and van Ossenbruggen 2019; Fickers 2020; Herrmann et al. 2023). Translating research data into a digital research artefact is therefore not merely a conversion between technical formats. Software shapes which modelled distinctions become visible and what researchers can do with them. Generic tools provide predefined representations and forms of interaction that may not correspond to a particular project’s data and scholarly practices. Researchers may consequently have to adapt their inquiry to assumptions developed for other purposes. Project-specific digital research artefacts remain selective, but allow those selections to be made and examined in relation to the project’s own data and purpose. ^p008

Scholar-Centred Design was developed in my dissertation as a method for translating scholarly perspectives into project-specific requirements for digital research artefacts (Pollin 2025a, 89–98). It combines requirements engineering with sustained engagement with scholars, their source material, and the modelling decisions through which the data were produced. The method developed through work on the *Bookkeeping Ontology*[^9] and DEPCHA,[^10] which used a shared model of historical accounting transactions to normalise and publish heterogeneous datasets (Pollin 2019). Two contrasting use cases showed why this interoperable basis was not sufficient. Work on the *Wheaton Day Book* required ways of tracing social relationships and forms of exchange through economic transactions, whereas work on the *Jahrrechnungen der Stadt Basel* required aggregation and comparison across administrative and temporal hierarchies. The shared representation of transactions supported both forms of inquiry but did not determine which distinctions should be foregrounded or which operations and interface structures were required for each investigation. ^p009

These requirements could be specified, but the project lacked the development capacity to realise them as functional, project-specific artefacts. Translating scholarly requirements into software has commonly involved collaboration between domain specialists and technical contributors, including Research Software Engineers (Edmond 2005; Baxter et al. 2012; Cohen et al. 2021; Kemman 2021). Such collaboration remains essential for artefacts that require dependable long-term operation, institutional integration, security, or supported use by third parties. The necessary expertise and resources, however, are not equally available to individual researchers and small projects (Carver et al. 2022). ^p010

LLM-based systems can reduce parts of the implementation work, making it more practicable to develop project-specific digital research artefacts within the research process. Not all relevant requirements can be determined in advance. Provisional artefacts make proposed distinctions and possible ways of working with the data available for examination. They can reveal problems in the specification, show what the data can and cannot support, and help researchers compare alternative approaches. **Implementation thereby becomes part of determining both how researchers wish to work with their data and what those data can support.** Promptotyping organises this iterative process through maintained project knowledge that guides human contributors and LLM-based AI agents as they develop and revise artefacts together, and into which findings from implementation are incorporated as revised project knowledge. ^p011

### 1.2 Context Engineering, Agentic Engineering, and AI Harnesses

LLMs have become increasingly capable of generating code and carrying out extended software-development tasks.[^11] Their effective and responsible use, where such use is warranted, requires more than selecting a capable model or formulating an effective prompt.[^12] Results also depend on the information available to the system, the organisation of the work performed by LLM-based AI agents, and the technical environment through which they interact with a project. These interdependent informational, organisational, and technical concerns are addressed here through Context Engineering, Agentic Engineering, and AI harnesses. ^p012

*Context Engineering* refers here to the systematic selection, organisation, maintenance, and provision of the information an LLM-based system requires for its work. It extends *Prompt Engineering*, understood here as the iterative development of prompts through changes to their content or the techniques applied to them (Schulhoff et al. 2024), from individual prompts to the wider informational environment in which they are interpreted (Mei et al. 2025). Within Promptotyping, this environment is organised around maintained project knowledge that records the project’s current understanding of its data and purpose, together with decisions and unresolved questions relevant to implementation. Context Engineering does not consist in placing all available material into a context window. It involves maintaining an inspectable body of project knowledge from which task-specific context can be selected and refreshed. ^p013

An *AI agent* is understood here as an LLM-based system that pursues a goal through a sequence of tool-supported actions and adapts to intermediate results (Sapkota, Roumeliotis, and Karkee 2026; Wang et al. 2024). Rather than responding only to an isolated input, an agent may inspect project resources, act on them, evaluate the results, and continue its work across several steps. *Agentic Engineering* refers here to the systematic organisation of this extended work. It concerns how tasks are decomposed and coordinated, how agents use tools and respond to intermediate results, when human intervention is required, and how their work is inspected and continued. The term is used broadly because Promptotyping agents work not only on code but also on the documents through which a project is described, directed, and assessed. ^p014

An *AI harness* is the technical environment through which an agent receives context, accesses project resources, uses tools, and obtains feedback. It mediates the relation between the model and the environment in which the work is performed. Agentic capability therefore arises from the combined model–harness–environment system rather than from the model alone (Zhong and Zhu 2026). A harness may provide controlled access to project files, execution environments, tests, and running artefacts while maintaining state and permissions. It thereby supplies the technical conditions under which Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering can be applied. ^p015

Promptotyping is not tied to a particular AI harness or level of delegated agentic work. Its earliest cases were conducted through iterative sequences of prompts, with human direction renewed at each step. Contemporary agentic development systems combine a high-capability LLM with an AI harness that gives the agent controlled access to a persistent project environment.[^13] When this environment is maintained as a Git repository, project documents, code, configuration, tests, and references to relevant research data can be kept together under version control. The unit of agentic work is therefore no longer an isolated prompt or code fragment, but the versioned project environment as a whole. Beyond increased model capability, the methodologically relevant change is the growing amount and variety of work that can be delegated within a persistent, tool-accessible project context between successive human interventions. ^p016

As AI agents become capable of completing increasingly extended, multi-step computational tasks between human interventions, the knowledge required to guide their work can no longer remain confined to an individual prompt, a transient conversation, or continuous human direction.[^14] It must persist beyond individual interactions while remaining available to project contributors for inspection and correction.[^15] ^p017

## 2. Promptotyping as a Method

Promptotyping developed through LLM-supported experiments conducted in research and teaching since 2023. Early work externalised and distilled project knowledge into task-specific representations that could guide implementation across successive interactions rather than within individual prompts.[^16] As coding agents became able to operate within persistent project environments, such as Git[^17] repositories, these representations were consolidated into an evolving and versioned project knowledge base from which agentic implementation and revision could proceed. The term *Promptotyping* was first used in my dissertation and subsequently elaborated in a blog post and an article published on a scholarly portal (Pollin 2025a; Pollin 2025b; Pollin 2026a). The present paper consolidates the method, while its specification, document templates, and revision history are maintained in the public [Promptotyping repository](https://github.com/DigitalHumanitiesCraft/Promptotyping). ^p018

### 2.1 Promptotyping and the Project Knowledge Base

*Promptotyping* is an iterative, knowledge-driven method for developing project-specific digital research artefacts from structured research data and maintained project knowledge through Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering. Its organising structure is an evolving and versioned project knowledge base. Project contributors curate this knowledge and define and oversee the bounded implementation and revision tasks carried out by LLM-based agents. The artefact is developed through the continuing interaction of maintained project knowledge, agentic execution, and human interpretation and judgement. Findings arising from implementation and examination are written back into the knowledge base, allowing subsequent work to proceed from this revised understanding. ^p019

The method is knowledge-driven because implementation proceeds not from the research data alone but from a maintained account of how those data are understood and are to be operationalised for the project’s scholarly purpose. The data remain the project’s source basis and are processed through explicit, inspectable operations. The project knowledge base records the project’s current understanding of those data, its scholarly purpose, and the decisions governing implementation. It is a knowledge base in the sense of an explicitly represented body of knowledge that guides an agent’s work (Russell and Norvig 2020), although it takes a semi-formal documentary form and supports no formal inference. Rather than replacing the research data or reproducing them in full, it provides a task-relevant account of their meaning and of the conditions governing their interpretation and operational use. ^p020

The project knowledge base consists of interrelated documents. These are bounded representations distilled from fuller research material, maintained for human inspection and revision, and made available for inclusion in agents’ task-specific working contexts. The following three document types are organisational heuristics rather than a prescribed file structure, and projects may name, combine, and distribute their functions as appropriate. ^p021

* ***Declarative knowledge documents*** state what the project currently takes to be the case and what the artefact is required to realise. A `data.md` may describe the structure, semantics, provenance, gaps, and uncertainties of the research data. A `requirements.md` connects scholarly practices with expected system behaviour, while a `design.md` records how the project’s modelled distinctions should be represented and made actionable in the artefact. ^p022

* ***Process knowledge documents*** preserve how the project’s understanding developed and why decisions were taken. A `journal.md` may record attempted approaches, failures, and unresolved questions, while consequential decisions may be retained in dedicated records. Together with version control and informative commit messages, these documents make changes and their rationale traceable across the project. ^p023

* ***Agent instruction documents*** translate maintained project knowledge into operational guidance for agentic work. Files such as `CLAUDE.md`[^18] or `AGENTS.md`[^19] may define the technical baseline, working procedures, constraints, and checks governing an agent’s work. Keeping these comparatively volatile instructions separate from more stable accounts of the data and scholarly purpose prevents temporary implementation directives from silently altering the project’s maintained understanding. ^p024

The project knowledge base may also be supplemented by *derived project artefacts*. These are regenerable outputs produced from a referenced project state through an identified process and stored in a format appropriate to their purpose. Their provenance and status may be recorded in document headers or accompanying metadata.[^20] They remain distinct from maintained project knowledge because they record derived observations rather than interpretations or decisions that project contributors have examined and adopted. ^p025

The persistent project knowledge base must be distinguished from an agent’s task-specific working context. The knowledge base preserves the project’s maintained understanding and decisions, while the working context contains the information and access required for a particular assignment. Relevant material may be drawn from maintained documents and derived project artefacts or obtained through retrieval and direct access to project resources. Retrieval-Augmented Generation may retrieve task-relevant material from larger collections and supply it as part of the working context (Lewis et al. 2020). This is particularly useful where those collections cannot adequately be represented through maintained summaries alone. These resources and access mechanisms may be combined, but they supplement rather than replace the maintained account of the project’s purpose, data semantics, and criteria of assessment. ^p026

Context Engineering organises the task-specific information and project access available to an agent, while Agentic Engineering structures how the agent acts within those conditions. The two are interdependent. A carefully maintained knowledge base does not determine an adequate course of action, and a well-organised agentic workflow cannot compensate for an inadequate account of the data or research purpose. Promptotyping is not tied to a particular documentary environment or mechanism for assembling working context. Project knowledge may be maintained in versioned files, an interlinked note system, or another inspectable and revisable form.[^21] Whatever arrangement is used, Promptotyping treats selection as part of Context Engineering rather than using accumulation as a substitute for it. The grounds for this restraint are examined in Section 2.2.3. The description does not replace the data, and access to the data does not replace the knowledge required to interpret them. An agent may therefore receive a distilled account and task-relevant selections from the research data, while inspectable computational operations process the complete dataset outside the model context. ^p027

The project knowledge base is therefore not prepared once and subsequently executed. It is established, examined, and revised through four recurrent forms of work that organise the development of a *promptotype*. ^p028

### 2.2 Forms of Work and Iteration

Promptotyping is organised around four recurrent phases of work: Preparation, Exploration, Distillation, and Implementation. These phases are analytically distinct but do not form a fixed or exclusively linear sequence. Their depth, order, and recurrence depend on the state and purpose of the project. Findings arising from Implementation may return the work to any earlier phase, most frequently to Distillation, where maintained project knowledge is revised. ^p029

**Figure 1. The Promptotyping method.** Preparation, Exploration, Distillation, and Implementation are recurrent phases of work. Findings from Implementation may return the work to earlier phases, most often to Distillation. The Critical Expert specifies and revises project knowledge, verifies the artefact, and accepts the iteration for its stated purpose, thereby establishing an identifiable promptotype. ^p030

#### 2.2.1 Preparation

Preparation assembles the source basis and research context from which subsequent work proceeds. It brings the relevant research material into an accessible project environment and makes its current status explicit. Analogue material may first require digitisation, while existing digital material may remain distributed across heterogeneous formats and levels of structure. Preparation does not require this material to be normalised, formally modelled, or placed directly in an agent’s working context. It requires the material to remain accessible for inspection and computational use, with its provenance and known limitations documented. ^p031

Preparation also gathers the scholar’s current understanding of the material and the project’s provisional purpose as documented in existing working materials. These may already express research questions or preliminary requirements, but their systematic formulation is not the primary purpose of Preparation. Exploration refines the relation between the available material and the intended scholarly work, while Distillation incorporates the resulting understanding into maintained project knowledge. Preparation is provisionally sufficient when the source basis and research context are digitally accessible and documented well enough to support systematic examination. ^p032

#### 2.2.2 Exploration

Exploration examines the prepared research material to understand its structure and limitations and to determine what the project knowledge base must represent. Once the source basis and research context are accessible, the material is examined for relevant patterns, variations, and unresolved problems. The purpose is not yet to formulate the maintained knowledge documents, but to establish what they need to contain for the project’s intended work. ^p033

The form of *Exploration* depends on the scale and structure of the material. A single text or a small collection may be examined directly, including through an LLM where appropriate. Large or heterogeneous collections may instead require computational profiling. For example, an LLM may generate code that extracts and aggregates structural information from thousands of TEI XML documents. The collection remains within the project environment, while the resulting profiles and selected examples can enter the agent’s working context. ^p034

The outputs of *Exploration* are provisional observations rather than maintained project knowledge. They become part of the knowledge base only through interpretation and Distillation. Exploration is provisionally sufficient when the material has been understood and documented well enough to determine what must be carried forward. ^p035

#### 2.2.3 Distillation

*Distillation* translates the understanding produced through Preparation and Exploration into maintained knowledge documents from which implementation and verification can proceed. It is the principal documentary operation of Context Engineering within Promptotyping. Distillation represents the project knowledge required for the current purpose while leaving the fuller sources and research data available for direct inspection and computational processing. ^p036

*Distillation* is not reducible to summarisation or context compression. A model’s nominal context capacity does not imply that all supplied information will be used reliably, and additional or poorly selected material may make relevant information more difficult to use (Liu et al. 2024; Hong, Troynikov, and Huber 2025). Distillation therefore does not seek merely to reduce token use. It creates an inspectable and selective representation that preserves the distinctions, conditions, and uncertainties required for adequate implementation and verification.[^22] It can consequently be understood as pragmatic modelling for human contributors and agent instances. The same material may need to be distilled differently when the project’s purpose or intended artefact changes. ^p037

The sufficiency of *Distillation* is practical rather than formal. A new contributor or agent instance, given the relevant knowledge documents and access to the project resources, should be able to reconstruct the project’s current logic and continue the assigned work without undocumented explanation. Difficulties in doing so can reveal omissions in the maintained account. Successful implementation, however, does not establish scholarly adequacy. A specification may be executable while resting on assumptions that cannot be justified. ^p038

#### 2.2.4 Implementation

Implementation makes maintained project knowledge actionable through LLM-supported development. This may proceed through iterative chat-based interactions in which contributors transfer prompts, code, and results between the model and the project environment. It is more effectively organised through an AI harness when the agent must work directly with project files, execute code, inspect intermediate results, and continue across several steps. In either arrangement, Implementation consists of bounded tasks through which proposed behaviour is realised and its consequences become available for examination. ^p039

Work proceeds through inspectable and versioned increments. Each increment should establish a runnable state that can be compared with the maintained project knowledge before further assumptions become embedded in the implementation. Early increments may prioritise making a transformation, interface, or workflow operational enough to support examination and discussion with project contributors. ^p040

Agentic implementation requires the project state to remain intelligible and testable as it develops. Generated code may require refactoring, while executable checks should be added or revised where relevant behaviour can be formalised. These practices are not merely subsequent technical clean-up; they preserve the inspectability of the implementation and make it easier to relate observed behaviour to the knowledge documents that guide it. Findings that alter the project’s maintained understanding initiate write-back to the relevant documents rather than remaining local corrections in the code. ^p041

Implementation is provisionally sufficient when the current artefact state is runnable, inspectable, and identifiable through versioning, so that it can support verification and discussion with responsible contributors. This state is not yet a promptotype; it provides the basis from which purpose-specific verification and acceptance can proceed. ^p042

A correction becomes methodologically consequential when it is incorporated into maintained project knowledge rather than confined to the current implementation. Write-back allows subsequent work to proceed from the revised understanding and is therefore not an additional phase, but the mechanism through which findings from Implementation become durable project knowledge. A Promptotyping iteration may contain several such cycles and concludes when the maintained project knowledge, resulting digital research artefact, referenced research-data state, and documented grounds of acceptance form a coherent and identifiable state for a stated purpose. **This accepted iteration state is a *promptotype*.** A runnable or plausible artefact alone does not meet this threshold if its relation to the project knowledge, data, and grounds of acceptance cannot be reconstructed. Acceptance remains the responsibility of the Critical Expert, understood as the person or group competent and accountable for judging whether the project knowledge adequately represents the research material and whether the artefact is suitable for its intended purpose. An agent may contribute proposals and assessments, but it cannot assume responsibility for their adequacy. ^p043

### 2.3 Applying Promptotyping: Complementary Project Cases

The preceding sections defined Promptotyping through its maintained project knowledge and the recurrent forms of Preparation, Exploration, Distillation, and Implementation. This section examines how these elements interact under different research conditions and at different stages of project development. The cases are presented as methodologically guided reconstructions of documented project practice rather than as complete histories or comparative evaluations. Each is analysed through the same elements: its initial project state, the interaction of the four forms of work, the findings arising from implementation, and their incorporation into revised project knowledge or purpose-specific acceptance. These elements provide a common analytical structure, not a fixed sequence. The analysis aims to show how Promptotyping operates under different research conditions and which methodological function implementation assumes in each case. ^p044

The first case brings together experimental workflows developed in the context of *Stefan Zweig Digital* (SZD)[^23] and the Zentralbibliothek Zürich project *Jeanne Hersch: Digitale Neuauflage der Schriften*.[^24] Both transform heterogeneous, multilingual source material into structured textual research data while keeping generated outputs and intermediate processing states available for inspection and correction. M³GIM[^25] provides a contrasting context in which early interfaces contributed to the development of a data model and capture practice for mobility information from the estate of the mezzo-soprano Ira Malaniuk. The Notker case[^26] examines a proposal-stage promptotype developed from a limited textual sample, editorial guidance, and an initial project description. Together, the cases show how implementation can support data production and curation, prospective modelling, and project formation under different research conditions. ^p045

#### 2.3.1 Agentic Data Production and Curation in the SZD and Jeanne Hersch Workflows

The experimental workflows examined here show how Promptotyping can organise agent-supported data production while keeping the relation among generated outputs, project knowledge, checking procedures, and responsible judgement inspectable. SZD works primarily with handwritten estate material, whereas the Jeanne Hersch workflow processes digitised printed writings. ^p046

*Preparation* brought the available sources and initial project requirements together in version-controlled working environments. In SZD, selected information from existing TEI records was inserted into the prompt supplied with each manuscript image. This included contextual details such as date, language, document type, writing instrument, and identified hand, while the classification of the object determined which task-specific transcription prompt Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite received.[^29] In ZBZ, the supplied source files, reference TEI, guidelines, and recorded project decisions were examined to distinguish relevant document and processing types and to formulate prompts for successive OCR, layout-recognition, and TEI-processing tasks. Preparation thereby translated existing scholarly knowledge into operational distinctions that could guide the treatment of particular sources. ^p047

*Exploration* examined how these arrangements worked in practice and where their assumptions failed. Researchers inspected selected facsimiles and generated outputs, while LLM-based agents investigated bounded samples by comparing images and transcriptions or tracing suspicious results back to their sources. Deterministic scripts extended this examination across larger sets of outputs and revealed recurrent structural or processing problems. In SZD, a controlled comparison showed that richer metadata could introduce information from the prompt into a transcription even when it was not visible in the image. In ZBZ, model-generated document classifications were treated as provisional hypotheses rather than authoritative descriptions. Agent findings likewise remained provisional until they had been connected to identifiable examples and interpreted in relation to the projects’ editorial aims. ^p048

The resulting understanding was distilled into the repositories’ maintained `knowledge/` directories. These documents record characteristics of the source material, processing requirements, annotation and checking conventions, methodological decisions, unresolved questions, and reasons for revising earlier approaches. In SZD, parts of this maintained knowledge are also processed by the build system and made accessible through the application. In ZBZ, the documentation records both the evidential value and the limitations of the supplied reference TEI, including phenomena that the available references do not represent and therefore cannot support as a basis for comparison. Distillation thus preserved uncertainty and negative findings rather than converting incomplete evidence into apparently settled requirements. ^p049

*Implementation* proceeded through successive versioned changes to prompts, processing routes, data representations, checks, and interfaces. Both repositories retain evidence of alternatives that were tested, restricted, replaced, or abandoned. Their current public deployments should therefore be understood as the latest inspectable states of documented development histories rather than as the direct execution of stable initial specifications. Findings from implementation were written back into maintained project knowledge, while revised knowledge informed subsequent implementation. ^p050

The examination of these implemented workflows required several forms of checking with different evidential scope and authority. In scholarly research, adequacy depends not only on conformity to specified requirements but also on the interpretation of sources, the modelling decisions through which they are represented, and the purpose for which the resulting data are intended. ^p051

* ***Deterministic verification*** tests conformity to formalised requirements through schemas, constraints, transformation tests, structural audits, and reproducible measurements. Its conclusions are limited to the properties encoded by the check and do not establish that the requirements themselves are adequate. ^p052

* ***Agentic review*** is a bounded, tool-supported investigation in which one or more LLM-based agents examine outputs, data states, implementations, or research artefacts against relevant sources, references, requirements, and criteria. It may involve locating project files, comparing sources and outputs, executing formal checks, investigating discrepancies, or coordinating specialised, parallel, or adversarial reviewers. ^p053

* ***Critical Expert verification and adjudication*** involves accountable examination of particular outputs against their sources and the resolution of cases that deterministic or probabilistic procedures cannot determine. It can confirm, correct, or reject previous findings and records who assumes responsibility for the resulting judgement. ^p054

* ***Scholarly validation*** assesses whether the representations, requirements, evaluation criteria, and artefacts governing the workflow are warranted by the research material and adequate for their intended scholarly purpose. ^p055

The term *agentic review* is used here in extension of emerging agent-based approaches to code review and other forms of tool-supported technical inspection. It also retains the broader sense of review as the criteria-guided and substantive examination of an artefact, as found in scholarly reviews of digital editions and resources. Whereas an LLM-as-a-Judge typically evaluates a supplied output under a given reference or rubric and returns a score, ranking, or judgement, an agentic reviewer can investigate a delimited part of a project by retrieving relevant materials, comparing several artefact layers, executing tools, and examining the grounds of apparent discrepancies. LLM-as-a-Judge can therefore be one operation within agentic review, but does not exhaust it. ^p056

The evidential value of agentic review depends on how the investigation is organised. Agentic Engineering determines how review tasks are delimited and decomposed, which project knowledge and sources are supplied, which tools and permissions are available, how several reviewers are coordinated, and when unresolved cases are escalated for human adjudication. Agentic review can consequently extend the scope and depth of inspection, but its findings remain probabilistic evidence rather than authorised verification, scholarly validation, or acceptance. ^p057

The differences among the four forms became consequential in both projects. In SZD, deterministic tests protected data contracts and the distinction between agent and human review states. Agentic review helped identify suspicious output patterns and directed attention to particular image–text discrepancies, while human examination corrected readings whose significance could not be determined through formal or statistical checks alone. Scholarly validation governed broader decisions about uncertainty, additions, transcription conventions, and TEI representation. In ZBZ, an earlier agent-screening process assigned approval labels even though no responsible contributor had granted approval. These labels were subsequently abolished, and agent findings were reclassified as provisional evidence pending operator adjudication. The capacity to inspect an output was thus separated from the authority to record it as verified. ^p058

The use of character error rate in ZBZ further illustrates the relation between deterministic verification and scholarly validation. Once the reference text, extraction rules, normalisation, comparison scope, and calculation procedure have been specified, CER can be calculated reproducibly. It nevertheless measures deviation from a selected reference rather than objective transcription correctness. The choice and quality of the reference, the treatment of corrections and annotations, the exclusion of particular textual phenomena, and the interpretation of the resulting value remain technical and editorial decisions. A deterministic metric can therefore verify a formally specified comparison without validating the scholarly adequacy of its specification. ^p059

Acceptance remains distinct from these forms of checking. It is the accountable decision through which an identifiable iteration state becomes a promptotype for a stated purpose (Section 2.2.4). Acceptance may accordingly be bounded: an artefact can be accepted as an experimental processing pipeline or handover state without being accepted as a completed or publication-ready scholarly edition. ^p060

The two workflows show that agent-supported data production becomes methodologically accountable not through automation alone, but through a reconstructable relation among sources, maintained project knowledge, versioned implementation, differentiated evidence, and responsible scholarly judgement. Their methodological significance lies not only in generated data and functional interfaces, but also in rejected assumptions, detected validation gaps, withdrawn approval states, corrected readings, and documented limitations that inform subsequent work. ^p061

#### 2.3.2 Prospective Modelling and Exploration in M³GIM

M³GIM shows how *Promptotyping* can operate while a project’s data model and requirements are still developing. The project investigates the mobility and professional life of the mezzo-soprano Ira Malaniuk through archival material from her estate. Rather than implementing a completed model, the project developed data capture and interface functionality alongside it. Implementation thereby became a means of examining and revising the emerging model in practice. ^p062

*Preparation* established a provisional basis for this work by bringing together available archival descriptions, initial records, modelling assumptions, and research interests. This basis was sufficient to begin examining the material and proposed distinctions, but neither the vocabulary nor the corpus was treated as complete. ^p063

*Exploration* proceeded through early functional representations of the emerging model. Rapidly developed interfaces allowed project partners to enter, display, filter, and compare information through a shared operational environment rather than discussing the model only through schemas and abstract descriptions. These interfaces made selected parts of the model actionable while leaving its categories, relations, and capture requirements open to revision. ^p064

*Implementation* made the consequences of provisional modelling and capture decisions available for examination. Categories could be assessed through the records assigned to them, the relations they permitted, the information they required during capture, and the representations they supported. Maps, timelines, networks, and related views showed how particular decisions organised the material and which interpretative claims those representations might appear to make. ^p065

Interaction with the interfaces revealed where distinctions could be supported by the available material, where categories remained ambiguous, and where additional information or qualification was required during capture. It also helped project partners examine which forms of inquiry the developing data could plausibly support and where a technically possible visualisation would imply a degree of precision, completeness, or certainty that the data could not warrant. ^p066

The resulting findings had to be interpreted before they could be written back. Some concerned the represented domain and required revision of the data model or capture practice. Others concerned how an otherwise adequate distinction should be made accessible and could remain within the operational or visual design of the interface. ^p067

Consequential findings were recorded in the project journal and decision register and distilled into revised project knowledge. Revised knowledge then informed subsequent modelling, capture, and implementation. The case therefore makes the prospective function of Promptotyping visible. A digital research artefact can participate in the formation of the model, capture practice, and requirements from which it is developed, provided that the consequences disclosed through its use are interpreted, documented, and incorporated into maintained project knowledge. ^p068

#### 2.3.3 Proposal-Stage Promptotyping in Notker

The *Notker* case shows how *Promptotyping* can support project formation before a complete corpus, production workflow, or funded development process exists. It began from a deliberately bounded source and knowledge base rather than from the materials required for a complete digital edition. Its purpose was to make a proposed editorial and technical arrangement functional enough to be examined and communicated within the preparation of a funding proposal. ^p069

*Preparation* brought together a limited textual sample, editorial guidelines, an emerging source model, and a Word document describing the proposed edition. These materials established the textual and organisational basis of the demonstrator while leaving corpus-wide modelling, transformation, and verification outside its stated scope. ^p070

*Exploration* examined how the proposed textual layers, annotations, translations, sigla, and modes of access might be represented in a functional artefact. The developing interface made it possible to compare alternative arrangements and identify requirements that remained implicit or ambiguous in the project description. ^p071

*Distillation* translated the emerging editorial and organisational account into requirements and design decisions that could guide transformation and interface development. Implementation then produced a bounded path from the initial material to a TEI representation and from that representation to a statically hosted Edition Interface. ^p072

The resulting artefact made the proposed relation among sources, textual layers, scholarly annotation, and modes of access available for inspection and discussion. It allowed the planned project to be encountered as a functional arrangement rather than only as a textual description. Implementation thus supported project formation by exposing the practical consequences of the proposal before a production corpus and workflow had been established. ^p073

The resulting promptotype was accepted as a demonstrator for a funding proposal, not as a completed or publication-ready edition. This purpose-specific acceptance did not establish corpus-wide philological adequacy and did not imply that the provisional transformation, model, or interface could be transferred unchanged into later production. The case shows that acceptance can stabilise a deliberately bounded project state for communication and decision-making without extending its claims beyond the materials examined and the purpose for which it was developed. ^p074

#### 2.3.4 From Complementary Cases to the Promptotype

The cases begin from different project states and foreground different functions of *Implementation*, but each maintains a reconstructable relation among maintained project knowledge, a referenced research-data state, the resulting digital research artefact, findings arising from its examination, and responsible judgement. SZD and ZBZ develop inspectable pipelines for producing and curating structured research data. M³GIM uses early interfaces to participate in data modelling, capture, and the formation of research requirements. Notker translates a proposal-stage concept into a bounded functional demonstrator. Promptotyping consequently prescribes neither a single project chronology nor one artefact form. ^p075

Maintained project knowledge guides *Implementation* without determining a single adequate realisation. Natural-language descriptions retain ambiguity, and different agent runs may realise the same requirement in materially different ways. The knowledge base therefore serves both as the reference from which implementation proceeds and as an account that can be revised when the resulting artefact exposes missing distinctions, unsupported assumptions, or inadequate requirements. Because formal conformity alone cannot establish the scholarly adequacy of such a realisation, Promptotyping requires the Critical Expert introduced in Section 2.2.4 to remain in the loop (Sterz et al. 2024). This responsibility extends beyond detecting errors in generated content to examining whether alternatives were excluded, conventions reproduced without justification, or absences concealed by an apparently coherent artefact. ^p076

Critical expertise may be held by one hybrid scholar-developer or distributed among contributors with complementary scholarly and technical competencies. Agentic work may likewise be divided among agents assigned to bounded components or checks, but this distribution does not transfer responsibility to them. Their assignments and permissions should remain explicit and auditable, and access to tools and project resources should be limited to the delegated task (Saltzer and Schroeder 1975; South et al. 2025). Their actions and outputs must additionally remain inspectable in relation to the project knowledge, sources, and criteria against which they are assessed. Increasing the number of agents does not reduce the need for responsible judgement and may increase the work required to coordinate and audit their actions. ^p077

An iteration is accepted when responsible contributors judge that it forms a coherent and identifiable state for a stated purpose, in the sense defined in Section 2.2.4. Acceptance is purpose-specific and does not imply that the project knowledge, research data, or artefact has become final. A promptotype may therefore be accepted as an exploratory interface, an internal production workflow, a proposal-stage demonstrator, a handover state, or a public research artefact. New sources, corrected assumptions, changed purposes, or improved checks may initiate another iteration and produce a later promptotype. ^p078

The accepted state must remain identifiable and reconstructable. Promptotyping does not require GitHub or Semantic Versioning, but the relation on which acceptance rests must remain recoverable through a repository release, archived deposit, or another durable reference. A renewed implementation using another model, harness, or project state constitutes a new iteration rather than a silent reproduction of the earlier promptotype. These complementary cases provide detailed reconstructions of Promptotyping but do not exhaust the documented practice from which the method was consolidated. The following chapter compares the wider corpus to identify recurring project conditions, methodological functions, artefact forms, and levels of findings. ^p079
